The Air Sim: a beginner's guide

For the ② Aero sim tab of Profile Studio. It covers how to run the sim, what you're looking at, and how to turn it into better shapes.

1What the sim is

It's a virtual wind tunnel. Air enters on the left at a steady speed and flows right past your silhouette. The computer tracks how the air speeds up, slows down, swirls, and pushes on the shape.

Two things are drawn on top of each other:

  • A colour field: every pixel shows a property of the air at that spot (speed, spin, or pressure).
  • Air particles: little tracers fired from the left edge that ride along with the air, like smoke in a real wind tunnel.

From the forces on the shape it calculates CD (how much it drags) and CL (how much it's pushed sideways, i.e. lift).

The one thing to remember The sim is 2D and runs at a low Reynolds number, so its numbers are not the real-world drag of your part. Use it to compare shapes against each other and to see where the flow goes wrong. For a real-scale drag number, use the revolved-body card below it.
How it works under the hood (optional)

The solver is a lattice-Boltzmann method (D2Q9). The tunnel is a grid of cells. Each cell holds 9 "packets" of air moving in 9 directions (still, 4 straight, 4 diagonal). Every time step, packets stream to the neighbouring cell and then collide, relaxing toward a local equilibrium. Averaged, that behaves like real viscous air. Packets that hit the body bounce straight back (a no-slip wall), and the momentum they hand over is the force on the body. A small turbulence model (Smagorinsky) keeps it stable at higher speeds.

2Quick start (2 minutes)

  1. Make a profile in ① Design (or pick one from Load example…; Ideal is the best place to start). Keep Mirror about x-axis on for a closed shape.
  2. Open ② Aero sim. Your shape appears as a light-grey body in the tunnel.
  3. Press ▶ Run (or Space).
  4. Watch the particles flow around it. Wait until Flow-throughs reaches at least 2–3. Before that, the flow is still "starting up" and the numbers are meaningless.
  5. Read CD. Switch Field to Vorticity and then Pressure to diagnose why it's that value.
  6. Change the shape in Design, come back, ⟲ Reset, run to the same flow-throughs, and compare.
TipHover over the tunnel. The top-right readout shows the local air speed |u|/U∞ and pressure Cp at the cursor. It's the fastest way to learn what the colours mean.

5The colour fields

Speed |u| / U∞

0 (stopped)1 = inlet speed1.8 (fast)

How fast the air is moving compared to the incoming wind. Teal-green ≈ free stream (1.0).

  • Dark spot at the nose: the stagnation point. Air slams into the front and stops. Normal and unavoidable.
  • Yellow/white bands over the top and bottom: air accelerating around the widest part (the "shoulder"). Normal. The more abrupt the shoulder, the brighter these get.
  • Thin dark layer hugging the surface: the boundary layer, where air is slowed by friction with the wall.
  • Dark streak behind the body: the wake, air that has lost energy. A narrow wake means low drag. A wide wake means high drag. This is the single most useful thing to look at.

Vorticity ω·H / U∞

blue = clockwise spindark = no spinred = counter-clockwise

How much the air is spinning locally. Spin is born at the surface (the boundary layer) and then carried downstream.

  • A thin blue sheet on top and a red sheet on the bottom of the body are normal: that's the boundary layer.
  • If those sheets peel off the surface early and billow outward, the flow has separated (bad, see patterns).
  • Alternating blue and red blobs behind the body are a vortex street: the body is shedding vortices and will shake side to side. You'll see CL oscillate.
  • Dark (black) everywhere else is calm, non-spinning air.

Pressure Cp

−1.2 (suction)0 = ambient+1.2 (pushing)

Pressure compared with the undisturbed air. Red pushes on the surface, and blue sucks on it.

  • Red cap on the nose (Cp ≈ +1 at the stagnation point): always there.
  • Blue over the shoulders: fast air means low pressure (Bernoulli).
  • The key check is the tail. On a good shape the pressure recovers back toward neutral or slightly red at the tail, which pushes the body forward and cancels some of the nose push. If the tail stays blue, the air separated and never recovered. That's pressure drag.
  • At an angle of attack, blue on top and red underneath means lift.

TipSpeed and pressure are mirror images (fast ↔ low pressure) outside the wake. Vorticity tells you where the damage started. Look at it first when CD is disappointing.

6Reading the particles

The particles are the "shoot air at it" view. Each one is released at the left edge and carried by the local air velocity.

You see…It means…
Long, straight streaksFast air. Streak length is proportional to speed.
Short streaks / bunched dotsSlow air, near the nose or in the wake.
Lines squeezing togetherAir accelerating (lines crowd around the shoulders).
Lines spreading apartAir decelerating (at the nose, behind the body).
Lines hugging the body all the way to the tailAttached flow. The goal.
Lines leaving the surface before the tail, with a gap behindSeparation. The gap is a recirculation bubble of trapped, churning air.
Particles circling / drifting backwards behind the bodyRecirculation: reversed flow inside the wake.
Wavy, snaking lines downstreamVortex shedding (a von Kármán street).
Particles vanishing at the surfaceThey got too close to the no-slip wall (where air speed → 0) or touched the body, so they're removed. Normal.
Best particle settings for seeing separationField = None, rake ≈ 20–30, trail ≈ 0.9. Recirculation shows up as a dark hole behind the body that the clean lines steer around.

The plot's vertical scale auto-zooms (the range is printed underneath), so a wiggle that looks huge may be tiny. Check the numbers.

Ballpark CD values (this 2D sim, Re ~1,000, default size)

ShapeTypical CD hereWhat you'll see
Flat plate facing the wind~2–3 (rough)Huge wake, strong shedding
Circle / cylinder~1.5–1.9 raw (1.87 at Re 100; published free-air 1.33, corrected readout 1.36)Separation at ~90°, vortex street

The raw values run 25–50% above free-air textbook numbers, mostly because of the tunnel walls (blockage). The CD blockage-corrected readout removes that. It is a Maskell-type correction, CD / (1 + 0.9·CD·h/H), calibrated against published cylinder, square and flat-plate data at Re 100, and it matches them to within about ±5% (see the README's validation section). It is least reliable for slender bodies, where the leftover error is grid resolution (+10–16% on a NACA 0012 at Re 1,000). The ranking carries over either way.

9Comparing designs properly

The sim is best as an A/B tester. Follow this so the comparison is fair:

  1. Lock the settings. Same Reynolds, resolution, body size and angle for every design you compare.
  2. Change one thing at a time (e.g. only the tail taper).
  3. ⟲ Reset, run to the same flow-through count (e.g. 4.0), and write down CD.
  4. Check the tail slope in the Design panel. Anything above ~20° is flagged red and almost always separates.
  5. When two designs are within ~5% of each other, confirm at a higher resolution. Small differences can be grid noise.
  6. Sanity-check with the revolved-body card at your real speed.
Design checklist: what "good" looks like
  • Particle lines stay attached all the way to the tail
  • Wake (speed field) is narrow, about the width of the tail or less
  • Pressure recovers at the tail (not blue)
  • No big alternating blobs in vorticity (or small ones)
  • CD history is flat or gently wiggling
  • Max tail slope under ~15–20°
  • Fit check: all known parts show clearance, none show interference
Shape lessons you'll discover quickly A rounded nose costs almost nothing, so the front shape matters surprisingly little. The tail is where drag is won or lost. Long and gentle beats short and steep. A blunt, cut-off tail makes a wide wake. A fineness ratio (L/D) around 3–5 is usually the sweet spot for a body of revolution.

10The revolved-body card

The card below the sim ignores the lattice sim. It treats your silhouette as the 3D body of revolution you'd get by spinning it about the x-axis, and it uses an engineering correlation (Hoerner) at your real speed and fluid.

Speed, FluidYour real operating condition (air or water at 20 °C). Lengths use the units from the Design tab.
Boundary layerTurbulent is the safe, conservative default. Laminar is only realistic for small, very smooth, slow parts (Re below ~5×10⁵).

Honest limits
  • 2D, not 3D. The sim treats your silhouette as an infinitely long extrusion (like a wing section), not the revolved body. Air can't go "around the sides", so wakes and drag are stronger than on the real 3D part.
  • Low Reynolds number. Real parts at real speeds have Re around 10⁵–10⁶ with thin, turbulent boundary layers. The sim is laminar-ish and syrupier.
  • Blockage. The body takes up to ~22% of the tunnel height.
  • So: trust the patterns and the ranking, not the absolute CD.

12Symbols & glossary

SymbolNamePlain-English meaning
U∞Free-stream velocityThe speed of the incoming wind, far from the body. Everything is measured relative to it.
|u|Local speedHow fast the air moves at a point. |u|/U∞ = 1 means "same as the wind".
Re, ReL, RehReynolds numberRe = U∞·L/ν. It's the ratio of the air's momentum to its stickiness. Low = smooth and syrupy, high = lively and turbulent. The subscript names the length used: L = body length, h = frontal height. The sim's slider uses whichever is longer.
ν (nu)Kinematic viscosityHow "sticky" the fluid is. Air ≈ 1.5×10⁻⁵ m²/s, water ≈ 1.0×10⁻⁶ m²/s.
ρ (rho)DensityMass per volume. Air ≈ 1.2 kg/m³, water ≈ 998 kg/m³.
q = ½ρU∞²Dynamic pressureThe pressure you'd feel from the wind hitting a flat hand. It's used to normalise forces.
CDDrag coefficientDrag ÷ (q × reference size). A size- and speed-independent measure of how "slippery" a shape is. Lower is better.
CLLift coefficientSame idea for the force perpendicular to the wind. Positive = up.
CpPressure coefficient(p − p∞)/q. +1 = full stagnation push, 0 = ambient, negative = suction.
ω (omega)VorticityLocal spin rate of the air. Shown as ω·H/U∞ (dimensionless). Blue = clockwise, red = counter-clockwise.
α (AoA)Angle of attackTilt of the body relative to the wind. Positive = nose up.
HFrontal heightThe body's height across the flow. It's the reference size for CD (2D version of "frontal area").
LLengthBody length along the flow. It's the reference for Re and CL.
L/D (fineness)Fineness ratioLength ÷ max diameter. Around 3–5 is usually lowest-drag per volume. (Not to be confused with lift-to-drag!)
τ (tau)Relaxation timeThe solver's internal viscosity knob, set automatically from Re. τ = 0.5 is zero viscosity. Close to 0.5 = near the stability limit.
MLUPSMega lattice updates / sSolver speed. Performance only.
Flow-through—One flow-through = the time for air to cross the whole tunnel once. The sim's natural clock.
Boundary layer—The thin layer of air slowed by friction next to the surface.
Separation—When the boundary layer can't follow the surface any more and lifts off, leaving a recirculating wake. The main cause of extra drag on bodies.
Stagnation point—The spot on the nose where the air comes to a complete stop (Cp = +1).
Wake—The region of slowed, disturbed air behind the body. Its width tracks drag.
Vortex streetvon Kármán streetAlternating vortices shed from each side of a blunt body. Causes oscillating forces.
Blockage—How much of the tunnel's height the body fills. More blockage speeds up the flow around the body and raises CD.
Free-slip wall—The tunnel's top and bottom: air can slide along them without friction, so they don't grow their own boundary layer.
CfSkin-friction coefficientFriction force per wetted area ÷ q (in the revolved card).
N1, N2CST class exponentsFrom the Design tab's CST fit. N1 shapes the nose (0.5 = round), and N2 shapes the tail (1 = sharp, 0 = blunt).
